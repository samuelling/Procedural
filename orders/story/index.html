What’s happening: one forest, five orders of magnitude

The same subject, a forest, written five times, each time with ten times the room: 200 bytes, 2 kB, 20 kB, 200 kB and 2 MB. Each rung is a single page that must fit its allowance whole, program and numbers and unpacker, with no stored images, models or sounds.

The bottom rungs are a problem of cutting. The top one turned into a problem of honest filling. Think of the same forest described as a haiku, a postcard, a field guide, a natural history, and an atlas. Each is not just longer than the one before: it is a different idea of what a forest is.

Order by order

0.2 kBOne formulaOpen ↗
198 of 200 bytes · 99.0% of the allowance

Here a forest is an impression: eighty firs in mist, and a tree is nothing but its number.

What is in it
One loop. Tree i stands at i·i·7 mod 900, is built from 28 stacked bars that widen downward, and is paler the further back it is (hsl(120,15%,90−i%)). Size, height, place and haze all come from the one index.
What had to go
  • Any <script> tag: the code runs from an SVG’s onload.
  • Looking anything up: the canvas’s id=c is used as a global.
  • Brackets: getContext`2d` is a tagged template.
  • Randomness, animation and colour beyond one hue: none of them fit.
198 bytes; there are two left.
2 kBOne shaderOpen ↗
1,670 of 2,000 bytes · 83.5% of the allowance

Here a forest is a view: every pixel works out which of ten rows of trees it is looking at.

What is in it
One fragment shader. Conifers with drooping tiers and ragged broadleaves, birches, boulders, mushrooms, grass, sun shafts with dust, falling leaves and a deer, under a camera drifting sideways forever. A hash and a few octaves of value noise stand in for every random choice.
How it fits
The shader source is stripped of comments and spaces and spliced into a few lines of WebGL setup. The page is a 163-byte bootstrap: it fetches its own file, inflates everything after itself with the browser’s own DecompressionStream, and runs it. Everything above it is raw deflate. That trick is what every rung from here up stands on.
What had to go
Geometry of any kind. Nothing exists between frames: no tree is anywhere until a pixel asks.
20 kBA genome and a grammarOpen ↗
19,960 of 20,000 bytes · 99.8% of the allowance

Here a forest is a grown thing: species are genomes, and trees are grown from them branch by branch in 3D.

What is in it
Sylva: four biomes and eight species, pipe-model trunks, moss as a rule, stones, logs, ferns, sun shadows, fog, light shafts, depth of field, wind and birdsong.
How it fits
It is built from the same source as the two rungs above it. TIER is a constant at build time, so every higher rung’s code folds away before minifying. It stands 40 bytes under its cap, so it is the tightest constraint on the whole project: every change since, overnight included, had to leave this page byte for byte the same.
The traps
Things that do nothing in this build still leave crumbs, and a few bytes is enough to break it. A stray let, an empty ...[], or a newline between two pieces of shader text each did. Overnight, a helper written as TIER > 3 && (…) left lu=!1 behind and reshuffled every minified name in the file. Only copying the code inline kept the rung identical.
200 kBThe measured Earth, seen coarserOpen ↗
195,154 of 200,000 bytes · 97.6% of the allowance
program, about 90 kBclimate classes in half degrees, about 14 kBmeasured grids, about 91 kB

Here a forest is the measured-Earth world (which held the 2 MB rung until the worlds replaced it), seen from further off. Every rule is here; only the measurements are coarser.

What is in it
Everything the 2 MB rung does: animals born, hunting, ageing and dying; trees growing, falling, burning and evolving; nine past ages; the lake and the cave; the zoom from a mountain range to the underside of a leaf, through a window into each next world. Click anywhere on Earth and the forest is grown from measured climate, ground and native trees, as before.
How it was filled
For its first two days this rung held a scene of 32 kB: a stream and a pool, weather, deer with needs, and nothing added to fill it. Then the question came back: why leave it a sixth full when the whole program fits? The program is about 90 kB. What fills the rest is the 2 MB rung’s own measured grids, each value replaced by the mean of a block and stored once. The page spreads each block back over its cells, and every rule downstream reads them as before. The block sizes were measured, not guessed. Climate in 2° blocks came to 140 kB alone. Climate in 3° blocks (about 330 km at the equator), with heights and tree ranges in 1.5°, left 8 kB spare.
What it costs
A place is its block. Innsbruck’s valley is 608 m up at 2 MB, and 796 m here, because its block takes in the mountains on either side. Its year comes to much the same: 8.4 °C and 979 mm above, 8.3 °C and 1,023 mm here. That is because temperatures are stored reduced to sea level and lapsed back to the ground they stand on. The horizon, which looks 120 km out, mostly falls inside one block, so the ranges around a plot are softer than at 2 MB.
2 MB(M)orders of Magnitude: the living Earth, from quarks to the cosmic webOpen ↗
1,943,335 of 2,000,000 bytes · 97.2% of the allowance
program, about 423 kBthe Earth’s ground and sea floor, about 968 kBclimate, lakes, tree ranges and the four rocky bodies, about 552 kB

Here a forest is a real place on the real Earth, at the real hour, in a world you can cross at any size: the same engine as the invented worlds below, given the Earth’s own maps.

Why
The invented worlds were asked to be less like science fiction: the Earth and the solar system instead, which means maps. The engine stayed; its invented continents were replaced by measured ones. Its rules (seasons, fire, beavers, herds, seas, caves, deep time, the zoom from quarks to the cosmic web) all run on the real ground now, and the six invented worlds are kept, on their own page and through a wormhole.
How a map becomes ground at every scale
The Earth’s heights are a 6 arc-minute grid (11 km), read bicubically, never dipping below the lowest of the four cells around a point on land, so a valley floor stays a floor and does not sink under the sea. Finer than the map, the old invented octaves are laid over it, scaled by how rough the map is there (the spread of heights round each half-degree cell), so the Himalaya is craggy and the Great Plains stay plain, and held back near sea level so the coast stays on the map’s line. Near the camera the map is its own Taylor series, worked out in double precision every frame, so walking on a grid 11 km wide is as steady as on the invented ground.
Real trees
Sixty-four genera, each a body plan from the invented worlds’ families (dome, cone, spire, palm, column, umbrella), with 671 species names. Nine genera come from range maps in North America and Europe, as in the measured Earth before; the rest by region and climate, and a few by place (redwoods only on the California coast, giant sequoias only on the Sierra’s west slope, saguaros only in the Sonoran Desert). Within a genus, which species stands where is drawn from its region’s list; that much is invented, and the page says so.
The real sky and the rest of the system
Positions of the planets from their Keplerian elements (JPL’s approximate table), the Moon from the largest terms of its theory, and every body’s turning from the IAU’s rotation models, all at the real date and time. Mercury, Venus, the Moon and Mars are worlds you can stand on: the Moon (LOLA) and Mars (MOLA) from half-degree maps, Venus (Magellan) and Mercury (MESSENGER) from one-degree maps, with invented craters finer than that.
Filling the last of the room
Three more measurements, chosen for where people look. The real stars: 10,526 from the HYG catalogue (Hipparcos, Yale Bright Star, Gliese), every one the eye can see and every one within 20 parsecs, each at its measured direction, distance, brightness and colour, so the night sky from any place is the real one and the constellations come apart as you fly out among them (invented stars fill in only below what the eye can see, until 100 parsecs out). The lights of the night side: NASA’s Black Marble 2016, in sixth-of-a-degree cells. And the ground round each of the 24 Go-to places on the Earth: ETOPO 2022 at 15 arc-seconds (450 m) over 44 km, stored as what the 6′ map misses there, which holds back the invented octaves it replaces; Yosemite’s valley, the Grand Canyon’s side canyons and the Eiger come out of it.
A living world
The herds were scenery: animals ambling round a fixed spot. Now they keep books, as the measured-Earth plots did: every animal is an individual, born to two parents and carrying their genes for size, speed, wariness and coat, built to its kind, and the grass, the insects, the songbirds and hawks, the herds and their hunters, the hares and foxes, the fish of the streams and the plankton, fish, hunters and whales of the sea are numbers that feed on each other a month at a time. Nothing is told what to do; selection falls out of who gets caught and who starves. The rates are field numbers where there are some (Yellowstone, the Serengeti, Kluane, Scotland), the dynamics the textbook ones. The trees near you joined in: each grows by the light and water it wins against its neighbours, dies, falls and is replaced from seed, and the deer eat the seedlings. The weather got a memory too: warmth a month behind the Sun, and snow that lies until it melts. All of it is kept in your browser between visits. Clocks up to ten years a second let you watch it.
Sound
Ported from the forest rungs below and still synthesised from noise and oscillators, with nothing recorded: wind, rain, thunder, fire, birds chosen by latitude, crickets and owls after dark, and footsteps by what is underfoot. It now follows the camera across scales: thinning with height, silent above the air and on the airless worlds, muffled under water and in the microscope. It is on by default and starts with the first click or key, the earliest a browser allows.
What it costs
1,943,335 bytes packed, 97.2% of the allowance. The maps were sized to what they change on screen; the last of the room went to the stars, the night lights and the ground round the Go-to places.
2 MB, beforeWorlds without a cutOpen ↗
75,073 of 2,000,000 bytes · 3.8% of the allowance

Here a forest is one place on a whole planet, and the planet is one of six. Nothing swaps as you change scale: from a globe in space to the cells of a leaf it is one height field, one set of trees, one sky, and you can walk, fly or fall through it at any size.

Why it replaced the measured Earth
The rung below this, at its best, was a set of separate worlds (a mountain range, a plot, a log, the moss, a leaf), each drawn its own way, joined by windows. Zooming meant cuts: a cloud became a flat sheet, a range became a different picture. The brief was asked again: what is 2 MB best spent on? The answer chosen was a world with no cuts at all, at every scale, then many of them.
How it stays one world
The ground is 40 octaves of noise on a sphere, from 4,000 km to a few micrometres, each kept in its own small lattice and measured from the camera, so a grain beside you is as precise as a continent. Trees stand on fixed sites 7 m apart and are drawn whole near you, as crowns farther off, and as the canopy's texture beyond. Ground cover, litter, stones and grains are lattices fixed to the planet. The clouds are a real shell around the globe. Each band dissolves into the next.
What a genome decides
Radius, gravity, the air (how thick, what colour it scatters), the star, the sea's liquid, rings and moons, the length of its day and year and its tilt, warmth and wetness, the colour of its aurora, whether anyone lives there, and its plants: ten to sixteen families, each a body plan with its own branching, droop, leaves, bark and climate niche, and hundreds of species in each, mutations of the family named from their genes. A region keeps a handful of them, so walking a few hundred km changes the forest. Gravity reaches everywhere: mountains, cloud height, tree height, wing length, how high you jump.
From the measured Earth
Most of what the rung below grew on real data comes back here as rules: seasons and leaf fall, snow that follows the cold, storms, aurora, herds and the packs that hunt them, a tree of life, a sea to dive in, caves, fire that runs before the wind and the forest that grows back, beaver ponds that fill and drain, and past ages, eight of them, back 450 million years, with the continents carried on drifting plates: ocean where they had not yet split apart, mountains where they collided.
Down to quarks, out to the cosmic web
The scale no longer stops at a micrometre or at the planet. Below, the camera keeps its own position in doubles from a point on the ground, and every shader measures from the camera (nucleons and quarks in femtometres, since metres squared would underflow a float), so the descent runs on from whatever is under the pointer: the pick buffer carries what each pixel is made of and which way it faces, and a leaf goes on to veins, cells, stomata and cuticle wax, bark to cork cells and cellulose fibrils, water to diatoms, snow to its crystals, sand, rock, soil and lunar dust to their grains, each to its own atoms, one atom’s electron cloud, its nucleus (built from alpha clusters; hydrogen’s is a single proton) and a proton’s quarks, down to a few attometres. Above, the star, its planets and their orbits, then stars on lattices of cells, each class twice as wide and four times as bright as the last, the galaxy as a density marched through, and galaxies and their web on the same kind of lattice by the megaparsec. Forty-four powers of ten in all, from 10⁻¹⁸ m to about 10²⁶ m. Below about a fifth of a micrometre the colours are conventions, said so on screen.
How the continents drift
Each planet is cut into twelve plates, each turning about its own pole at a steady rate. A point in a past age is asked which plate, turned back to today, would cover it. None: ocean floor. One: that plate’s own ground. Two: the one further inside its own plate rides over, and the ground rises in a belt along the front. Only the coarse ground moves (continents down to hills 32 m across); the finer octaves are the same everywhere, so they need not. The plate under the camera keeps the usual near-field precision, so a grain is still exact 300 million years ago.
What it cost
Nothing measured: these are invented planets, so the page is all program, 75,073 bytes packed, 3.8% of the allowance.
2 MB, before thatThe measured EarthOpen ↗
1,747,123 of 2,000,000 bytes · 87.4% of the allowance
program, about 89 kBKöppen climate classes, about 36 kBmeasured grids, about 1.62 MB

Here a forest is a place in the world, in time. It has a history: animals are born, hunt, age and die, and trees grow, fall, burn and evolve over centuries. It has a whereabouts: click anywhere on Earth and its measured ground, months, humidity and wind, and, where range maps cover it, its native trees, decide what grows. And it holds together from a mountain range 640 km across down to the underside of a leaf.

The problem
By the evening before, everything here had been written as rules: lives and succession, fire, beavers, nine past ages, caves, the powers-of-ten zoom. It came to 113 kB, so the whole program would have fitted in the 200 kB rung with room to spare (later, it was put there; see above). More rules would not have filled 2 MB honestly. Padding was out, so the remaining 1.88 MB had to be things that are true.

What the world is, at every order

Read upward, the rungs show how much has to be written down before a forest is anywhere at all. At 200 bytes a tree is its index. At 2 kB it is a distance worked out per pixel. At 20 kB it is a genome grown into a body. At 200 kB it has every rule the project has written, and an address seen from 300 km off. At 2 MB the address dissolves into a whole planet, and the planet is the real one: there are no more rungs inside the rung, just one world you can cross at any size, its sky and neighbours where they really are, and six invented worlds through a wormhole. (Before that, the 2 MB room held those invented worlds on their own, and before them the measured Earth; both are still open.)

The rule all five share is the one in the brief: no pictures of a forest, only ways of making one. The lower rungs prove how little that takes. The top one tests whether, given room, the making can be made true.

The overnight dialogue

The measured-Earth 2 MB rung was filled by an argument. Two Claude agents took turns on each round, then the main session built what they agreed, checked it, published it, and wrote what it changed and why.

AshProposes. A naturalist and builder: what would make the scales one world, ranked by how much each buys per byte.
RowanChecks. A sceptical engineer: reads the code, quotes line numbers, fetches and packs the data to measure it, and pushes back on anything fake.
BuilderThe main session. Builds the agreed list, says where it departed and why, and what was and was not seen.

The format earned its keep. Rowan caught a shader that never read the uniform Ash was counting on. He measured data that came in at two to four times Ash’s guesses: humidity and daily range, guessed at 40–60 kB each, packed to 226 kB together. And he warned that Innsbruck’s January would come out too warm for snow before any render showed it. Ash found that dew formed at every dawn everywhere, even in the Atacama, and kept pulling the work back to the brief: the scales felt like separate slides, and every round had to make them less so. The Builder found its own worst bug: a sea at negative height that sent the range’s flood fill round forever and hung the page.

Still open

  • Is a relief model a model? ETOPO calls itself a Global Relief Model. It is a grid of measured heights, and the page uses it as data, but it is the borderline case under the "no stored models" rule.
  • SoilGrids is a machine-learning prediction of soil. It is left out until that is ruled on.
  • The window, designed by Rowan in round 5, was built the morning after: going in, the next world now appears inside a disc on the ground where its patch lies, at true scale and under the same sun, and takes over when it fills the view. Going out still dissolves.
